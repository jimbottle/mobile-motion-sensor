## Wiring to verify

| Sensor | Ground | Power | Signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| BS412 and BL412 | pins 1 and 2 to GND | pin 3 to D2 | pin 4 to D1 |
| 4871 | pin 1 (minus) to GND | pin 3 (plus) to D2 | pin 2 (O) to D1 |

- D2 is used as switched power so that disarming can cut the sensor off. D1 is a plain input, since all three sensors drive their output high and low.
- Pin 2 of the BS412 and BL412 at ground selects the shortest on-time, 2 seconds.
- Identify pin 1 from the drawing on page 7 of the BS412 datasheet before soldering. A rotated sensor gets power on the wrong pin.
- The BS412 and BL412 must never see more than 3.3 V. The datasheet's absolute limit is 3.6 V.
- One 0.1 µF capacitor goes across the sensor's power and ground pins. One 100 µF capacitor goes across the Puck's 3V and GND pads, stripe to GND, and stays off the D2 line.
- If the 4871 misbehaves on D2, power it from the 3V pad.
- Bench rig: four jumper-wire halves soldered to the Puck's GND, 3V, D1 and D2 holes, with their pin ends in the breadboard.

## Architecture

### What constrains it

- Reviewing agent: every app switch calls load(), which resets the interpreter. Only boot code and widgets re-run. The watch side has to be a boot file or a widget, never a normal app.
- my-bangle/docs/bangle2-limits.md says the watch holds one Bluetooth connection at a time. The reviewing agent notes this was measured for incoming connections only. An outgoing connection alongside Gadgetbridge has not been measured on this watch at firmware 2v29.
- Espruino's developers describe a limit of one outgoing plus one incoming connection. If that holds, a held connection can never serve two sensors.
- The owner wants room for a second sensor.

### Candidate A, the lead: broadcast and scan

- The sensor accepts no connections in normal use. It broadcasts its state in advertising packets.
- While armed it sends a steady heartbeat. On a trigger it bumps a counter and advertises fast for a few seconds.
- The watch scans from boot code or a widget. It keeps the last counter seen for each sensor ID and buzzes when a counter moves.
- A sensor that was last seen armed and then goes unheard raises a different buzz.

Why it leads: it needs no connection slot, so the one-connection limit and the reset on every app switch stop mattering. It serves any number of sensors. It lets the sensor refuse all connections outside a maintenance window, which removes the stranger problem without a stored secret.

What can sink it is the scanning cost on the watch. Nordic support quotes about 6.5 mA of receive current while scanning on this chip family, against roughly 0.3 mA of everyday draw reported for the Bangle.js 2. Rough arithmetic says continuous scanning would empty a 200 mAh watch battery in about 30 hours. Options to test:

- Duty-cycled scanning. Espruino's developers say NRF.setScan takes window and interval in milliseconds since 2v22, both defaulting to 100, which is continuous.
- Two scan rates. A slow discovery rate while no armed sensor is known, and a fast alert rate while one is armed. PIR warm-up after arming can hide the discovery delay.
- An owner-facing listen toggle on the watch, if the automatic rates cost too much.

A starting point for the timing, from first principles: the scan window should exceed the burst's advertising interval plus 10 ms, and the burst should outlast the scan interval. Worst-case radio latency is then about one scan interval, so the scan interval has to fit inside 2 seconds minus the sensor's own trigger delay.

### Candidate B, the fallback: held connection

- One sensor only. The Puck exposes a custom service with a trigger-count characteristic that notifies. The watch connects as a central from boot code and reads the count on every reconnect.
- It needs measurements A does not: whether an outgoing link coexists with Gadgetbridge at 2v29, whether it survives an app switch, how long a reconnect takes, and what connection interval is negotiated.
- It needs a "release the sensor" control on the watch, because the computer cannot reprogram a Puck while the watch holds its only slot.
- It needs stranger protection, because one stray connection takes the slot and silently stops alerts.
- It cannot serve a second sensor. Choosing B needs the owner's sign-off.

### Candidate C, the last resort: phone relay

Sensor to phone to Gadgetbridge to watch. This was the owner's first idea. It adds an Android component nobody has scoped, and it puts the phone in the alert path. Raise it only if A and B both fail.

### The gate

Choose A if it meets the 2 second bar end to end and the owner accepts its measured cost in watch battery. Otherwise take B to the owner with the numbers. Send the measurements through a handoff. The owner decides.

## Protocol

Write docs/protocol.md in the new repo before the firmware. "Pushes a notification" is not a specification.

Requirements:

- A version field, so the watch can reject a payload it does not understand.
- A sensor ID, so two sensors never collide.
- A trigger counter that only moves forward and wraps predictably.
- A boot ID, so the watch can tell a restarted sensor from a wrapped counter.
- State flags that let the watch tell "switched off" from "lost". A sensor that is disarmed should say so before it goes quiet.
- Battery level.
- No secrets in the payload.
- Under B, named 128-bit UUIDs for the service and the count characteristic. Never the Puck's default serial console.

A starting point for the broadcast payload, yours to change. It fits in manufacturer data, which Espruino can filter on in a scan.

| Byte | Field |
|---|---|
| 0 | Protocol version |
| 1 | Sensor ID |
| 2 | Boot ID, random at power-up |
| 3 | Flags: armed, settling, disarming, maintenance, low battery |
| 4 to 5 | Trigger counter, little-endian |
| 6 | Battery percent |
| 7 | Tenths of a second since the last trigger, capped at 255 |

The last field lets the watch log radio latency for every alert without synchronised clocks. Document which company ID the manufacturer data uses. Espruino's own is 0x0590.

## Security and reprogramming

- Espruino's documentation and forum say a stock Puck has no access control. Anything in range can connect and reach the JavaScript console without a pairing code.
- Field build: no console reachable and, under A, no connections accepted. Espruino documents NRF.setServices(undefined, {uart:false}) for removing the console and connectable:false for advertising. One forum thread reports that connectable:false stopped advertising altogether on a custom board, so confirm the sensor still advertises.
- Maintenance window: a long press on the Puck's button opens the console for a few minutes, with an LED pattern to show it, then closes it again. Physical presence is the credential, so nothing secret exists.
- Development build: keep the console open until the firmware settles, so each upload does not need a button press as well as a chooser click. Make the difference between a development build and a field build visible at boot.
- Recovery: Espruino documents two button-at-power-up paths. One skips saved code without erasing it. The other, a hard reset, erases saved code and bonding data. Write both into a runbook before the first locked build is flashed.
- Never use the button at power-up for a firmware feature, since Espruino already uses it.
- If any design needs a passkey or password, it is a credential. It lives in 1Password as mobile-motion-sensor-<service> and never in the repo, which is public.
- A broadcast protocol can be faked or replayed by anyone nearby. A counter that only moves forward makes old replays harmless, but a forger could still advance it. Proposed default: accept and document the risk. This is an owner decision.

## Puck firmware requirements

- A short press toggles armed, with different LED confirmations for arming and disarming.
- Arming powers the PIR from D2. Disarming cuts it.
- The BS412 datasheet gives no warm-up time. Measure it, and ignore the output until the sensor has settled. If warm-up is too slow, leave the PIR powered and ignore its output while disarmed.
- A trigger is a rising edge on D1 while armed and settled. The BS412 holds its output high for 2 seconds and then ignores motion for 2.3 seconds, so expect at most one trigger about every 4.3 seconds.
- On a trigger: counter plus one, then the fast advertising burst, then back to the heartbeat.
- On disarm: say so in the broadcast for a short while, then go quiet or slow.
- Report battery level.
- A long press opens the maintenance window.
- Measure total current in each state: disarmed, armed and idle, and during a burst. Espruino's figures for comparison are 22 µA for non-connectable advertising at 375 ms and 40 µA for connectable advertising.
- Under B, the watch as central sets the connection interval, so Espruino's 40 µA connected figure may not hold. Measure total Puck current with the watch connected.
- Keep the state machine, counter and debounce logic testable in Node with mutants. A device check is still required before anything is closed.

## Bench plan once the parts arrive

Each step is an owner handoff with a runbook. Batch them into as few sessions as you can.

1. Place the order.
2. Unbox and power the Puck. Read the 3V pad with a multimeter while it advertises. The earlier review predicted 2.8 to 2.9 V under load, which is unverified.
3. Solder the four jumper pigtails and build the breadboard rig.
4. BS412 sanity check, with the Puck's LED following D1. Pass: it trips on a walk-by and stays quiet for 5 minutes in an empty room.
5. Range walk test. Cross the sensor's view at 5, 10 and 15 ft, then walk straight at it. Pass: every crossing at 15 ft trips it. Repeat with the BL412 and the 4871 and keep the best.
6. Radio false triggers. Tape a card over the lens, force the radio to burst, and count trips for 30 minutes. Pass: zero. If it fails, add the 100 µF capacitor, shorten the wires and move the sensor away from the Puck's aerial.
7. End-to-end latency. A person steps into view and the watch buzzes within 2 seconds, over at least 20 trials. Split the result into the sensor's delay and the radio's.
8. Stress. Out of range and back, app switches on the watch, Gadgetbridge connected throughout. Read the Storage log afterwards. Pass: no trigger lost, and no lost-sensor buzz in normal use.
9. Warm-up after arming.
10. Repeat steps 5 to 7 on a partly used coin cell.
11. Current. Total Puck current in each state, and the sensor's own draw on the D2 line. Expect about 10 µA for a BS412.
12. Measure the chosen sensor's lens with calipers. Adafruit lists no lens size for the BS412 or BL412.
13. Print the case, assemble, and repeat step 5 with the sensor inside it.

If all three sensors fail step 5, stop and tell the owner. The fallback is the Panasonic EKMB1203111, which was not ordered. Its facts are in the claims table.

## Case constraints

- The Puck's bare board is 29 mm across. Adafruit lists 8.3 mm thick and Espruino 9 mm, so allow 9 mm plus clearance.
- Keep the board a loose fit. Espruino warns that forcing it into a case can damage the aerial.
- The lens has to poke through the wall. Printed plastic blocks the infrared a PIR needs.
- The 4871's lens is 13.8 mm across. Cut its hole at 14.3 to 14.5 mm, since printed holes come out undersized.
- Side by side, the Puck board and a 13.8 mm lens span about 43 mm, inside the 2 in limit of 50.8 mm.
- Leave access to the Puck's button and a removable back for the coin cell.
- A snap-on hood narrows the view for threshold use.
- A printed clip needs no hardware. Magnets were not ordered.
